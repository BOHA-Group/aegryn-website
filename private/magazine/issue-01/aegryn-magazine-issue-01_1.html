Five moments in the life of a company
Every company passes
through the same five doors.

An industrial manufacturer, a SaaS scale-up, an automotive Tier-2 supplier, a healthcare group and a construction firm do not share a business model. They share a lifecycle. This issue is organised around it.

01
Launch and structuring

The EU business economy recorded a 10.5 percent enterprise birth rate against an 8.5 percent death rate in 2023 (Eurostat, 2026): more companies open than close, and most of the closures happen young. For an industrial SME as much as a software company, the first eighteen months decide who owns what, who signs what, and whether the books can be read by a stranger. Most of what goes wrong later is decided here.

02
Growth and scale

Capital is not scarce: European private equity and venture investment reached 135 billion euros in 2025, the second-best year on record (Invest Europe). What is scarce, from a SaaS scale-up to a logistics group, is an organisation that survives its founder's calendar: a management team, a compliance function, a technology stack the company actually controls.

03
Restructuring and pivot

Business registrations fell and bankruptcies rose across the EU in the second quarter of 2026 (Eurostat). The automotive supply chain is living this now, as Tier-2 suppliers built for combustion engines re-tool for electrification. A pivot done early is a strategic choice. Done late, it is a restructuring, with the same decisions taken under a bank's timetable rather than the owner's.

04
Acquisition and external growth

EMEA deal value rose 87 percent in the first half of 2026, its best first half since 2007 (Mergermarket). Healthcare and pharmaceutical consolidation led 2025 private-equity activity in value terms (Invest Europe). Buying is easier than integrating. The deals that create value are the ones where the buyer already knew, before signing, which people, systems and contracts they were actually acquiring.

05
Transfer and succession

Around 545,000 German Mittelstand firms seek a successor by 2029, and 569,000 plan simply to close (KfW, January 2026). In Switzerland, 32 percent of SMEs plan an ownership transfer within five years; at current success rates roughly 12,000 handovers a year will fail (UBS and University of St. Gallen, 2026). Construction, industrial and retail trades carry a disproportionate share of that backlog. Succession is a national economic question that is settled one company at a time.

Primer·Five Words Worth Knowing
The vocabulary, plainly defined
Five words
worth knowing.
ARR

Annual Recurring Revenue. The yearly income a software company can reliably count on, from customers who pay every month or year. The number most buyers start from.

Multiple

How many years of revenue a buyer pays upfront. At 4× ARR, a buyer pays four times the annual revenue, all at once, today, for the right to own everything.

Due Diligence

The buyer's investigation of everything the seller has claimed. Financial records, code, legal ownership, customer contracts. The process that separates a fast close from a slow one.

Earn-Out

A portion of the sale price paid later, based on whether the business hits agreed targets after the sale closes. A bridge between what the buyer will pay today and what the seller believes the company is worth.

NRR

Net Revenue Retention. Whether existing customers spend more or less than last year. Above 100% means growth from the existing base alone. One of the strongest signals of a healthy business.

No investors. No co-founder. No plan B. Just a laptop, an idea she had been circling for two years, and a generation of tools that let one determined person do what used to require a department.

She built the first version in six weeks, nights and weekends, then full-time once the fear of staying became larger than the fear of leaving. The product worked. Customers paid. She was, understandably, proud of the pace.

Tech and AI·Building From Anywhere
Remote by Choice
Building from a
café in Lisbon.

Why a city of half a million people became Europe's most-discussed founder address, what it actually offers, and what it no longer does.

He moved from Amsterdam eighteen months ago, told himself it was temporary, and never left. His mornings look the same most days: coffee at a corner table by eight, three hours of focused building before the café fills up, then a walk to the river to think through whatever went wrong. He is one of thousands. The pattern is now old enough to have a history. It started with a conference. Web Summit left Dublin for Lisbon in 2016 and has stayed since, with the city committing to host it into the late 2020s. What followed was less an accident than a policy: a municipal Unicorn Factory programme launched in 2021, the Hub Criativo do Beato converted from a military bakery into a campus, and a national residence permit for remote workers, the D8 visa, introduced in October 2022 for non-EU nationals earning above a defined monthly threshold. The results are visible in the cap table of the country. Farfetch, OutSystems, Talkdesk, Feedzai, Remote and Sword Health all reached unicorn status with Portuguese founders or Portuguese roots, a remarkable count for an economy the size of Portugal's.
Alfama, 9am. The light that keeps founders from going back.

Tech and AI·Building From Anywhere

His entire product runs on tools that did not exist five years ago: coding assistants, design generators, a customer support system that handles most questions before a human ever sees them. What used to need a team of eight now needs him, a good pair of headphones, and a city that costs a fraction of what Amsterdam did.

The arithmetic still favours Lisbon against Zurich, Amsterdam or Paris on rent, on salaries for the first hires, and on the cost of a desk in a shared space. But it favours it less than it did. Lisbon posted some of the fastest rent increases of any European capital between 2021 and 2023, and the strain on housing pushed the government to act.

What changed in the rules

Two of the incentives that drew the first wave have since been withdrawn or narrowed. The real-estate route into the golden visa was closed in 2023. The Non-Habitual Resident regime, a ten-year flat tax that had made Portugal unusually attractive to foreign professionals, was shut to new applicants from 2024 and replaced by a narrower scheme aimed at scientific research and innovation roles.

For a founder arriving today, the offer is therefore different from the one advertised in 2019. The community, the conference calendar and the English-speaking service layer remain. The tax arbitrage largely does not.

Why it still holds

What remains is the part that matters for a company meant to last. A time zone that overlaps both New York's morning and Central Europe's afternoon. A stable EU legal framework for contracts, employment and data. Direct flights to every major European buyer. And a founder population dense enough that a technical co-founder, a first client or a lawyer who has seen a cross-border SaaS sale can be found within a week.

This is not a remote-work trend piece. It is the new default for a generation of founders: build somewhere that works, because for the first time nothing structural is stopping you, and the structural things are what an acquirer will eventually check.

Tech and AI·Worth Knowing
A Correction Worth Making
Switzerland has no
nomad visa. The real advantages are elsewhere.
Despite what circulates online, Switzerland has no digital-nomad visa. Founders building from Lausanne or Geneva use standard residence or business permits, and non-EU nationals face cantonal quotas. The country's actual advantages are elsewhere: a predictable cantonal tax environment, the proximity of EPFL and ETH Zurich, two of the world's highest-ranked engineering institutions, and a dense network of first-loss funders such as FIT in Vaud or Venture Kick nationally. The money followed: venture investment in Swiss startups rose 23.9 percent to 2.95 billion francs across 354 rounds in 2025, the first increase since 2022, with early-stage funding climbing from 864 million to more than 1.4 billion francs (Swiss Venture Capital Report 2026, startupticker.ch and SECA). Two roles now recur in Swiss hiring briefs. AI Governance Lead: owns EU AI Act risk classification and documentation before a regulator asks. Fractional CTO: technical leadership rented by the month, common among Series A companies too lean to hire full-time.

Tech and AI·What Actually Changed
The Freedom Ledger
Nine to five
is optional now.
The best ideas rarely arrive at a desk.

Ask any founder who built solo in the last two years what actually changed, and none of them mention artificial intelligence first. They mention their mornings. Their ability to take a Tuesday off. The fact that "office" now means wherever the wifi is reliable and the coffee is better.

The tools do the repetitive work, the first draft, the boilerplate, the parts of building a company that used to eat entire weeks. What is left for the founder is the part that was always the actual job: deciding what matters, talking to customers, and having the nerve to ship something before it is perfect.

Freedom, in this context, is not a slogan. It is a Tuesday afternoon spent by the pool with a laptop open, half-working and half-not, because for the first time the two are not opposites.

The statistics lag the anecdotes, which is usually how it goes. In 2025, 20 percent of EU enterprises with ten or more employees used at least one AI technology, up from 13.5 percent the year before. The most common use was analysis of written language, at 11.8 percent of enterprises, followed by image or audio generation at 9.6. Denmark led at 42 percent, Finland at 38. Her Tuesday afternoon is, statistically, still a minority pursuit (Eurostat, 2025).

Build·The Habit That Changes Everything

He did not set out to build a company that would sell well. He set out to build one that would not embarrass him. So from the very first month, every contractor signed a proper agreement. Every invoice got filed the same day. It felt excessive at the time, a single person running a company nobody had heard of, filing paperwork like it mattered.

Ten years later, it mattered enormously. When a buyer's team opened his records, they found exactly what they expected to find: nothing missing, nothing to explain away.

"The due diligence team said they had never seen a data room this clean. I thought it was just how you ran a serious business."European founder, acquired 2026

The habit he started out of simple discomfort with mess became, without him planning it, the entire reason the sale closed in weeks instead of months. This is the part almost nobody tells new founders: the boring administrative choices you make in year one are not separate from the exciting part of building a company. They are the same decision, made early, before anyone is watching.

Why the paperwork is priced
The CMS European M&A Study 2026, built on 601 private deals advised in 2025, shows how a buyer converts uncertainty into price. Purchase price adjustments appeared in 48 percent of transactions, unchanged for a decade; where they were absent, a locked-box price fixed on a past balance sheet featured in 54 percent. Earn-outs grew again, warranty periods lengthened and lower liability caps became rarer. Every one of those mechanisms exists to protect a buyer against what the seller cannot document. A clean file does not remove them. It removes the reasons to use them aggressively.

Build·Documented Before Built
A quality system written before the first prototype. Unusual, and decisive.
Two engineers,
one regulator,
and a binder.

They had a sensor, a patent application and eighteen months of runway from a first-loss loan. What they did not have, in 2020, was a product. Their first hire was not an engineer. It was a quality manager who had spent a decade inside a large device manufacturer, and whose first act was to open an ISO 13485 quality system for a company with two employees and no revenue.

Their investors thought it premature. The regulator did not. The EU Medical Device Regulation had applied since May 2021, and every design decision, every supplier, every test result would eventually have to sit in a technical file a notified body could read. Writing it from day one cost them roughly a fifth of their engineering time in the first two years.

It also meant that when the file was needed, it existed.

Build·Documented Before Built

The buyer arrived in 2025, a mid-sized European device group looking for a diagnostic line it could push through its own distribution. Its diligence team expected the usual: a technical file assembled in the six weeks before the sale, gaps in supplier qualification, design history reconstructed from email. They found a file that had been audited twice, a risk-management record maintained since the first prototype, and a post-market surveillance plan for a product that had only just launched.

Technical diligence took three weeks instead of the twelve the buyer had budgeted. The price reflected the nine weeks saved and, more importantly, the absence of a remediation programme the buyer would otherwise have had to fund before the MDR transition deadlines of December 2027 and December 2028 (Regulation 2023/607).

A tech asset
doesn't have to be
a startup.
Aegryn Founding Team

Most conversations about technology assets assume a startup, young, venture-adjacent, growing fast. We see something different every week: family businesses, small industrial companies, service firms, quietly digitising themselves into something transferable and valuable.

The scale of that pool is easy to underestimate. Eurostat's 2025 Digital Intensity Index found that 71 percent of EU SMEs reach at least a basic level of digitalisation, but only 9 percent reach a very high level and 27 percent a high one. Nearly two thirds of European small and medium businesses still sit at a low or very low level, well under their addressable ceiling. That gap is not a weakness to fix before a sale. For an advisor, it is the largest pool of under-documented transferable value in the European mid-market, structurally larger than the venture-backed universe most coverage focuses on.

We think this is the more interesting story in European tech right now. Not the next unicorn, but the third-generation manufacturer whose granddaughter built a client portal. Not the funding round, but the fiduciaire who insisted on clean books from month one. The unglamorous version of building, which turns out to be exactly the version that sells well. It rarely makes a pitch deck. It shows up, consistently, in the data room.

Build·The Bookkeeper Who Saved Six Weeks

She hired a proper bookkeeper in year two, long before she could comfortably afford it. Every friend told her to wait. She had watched a previous employer spend months untangling records during a sale, and she never wanted to live that.

For six years, her books closed within 48 hours of every month-end: clean, consistent, boring in the best possible way. When her sale process began, the buyer's team reviewed three years of records in four days.

On a comparable deal that same quarter, a founder without this habit spent six weeks doing the same work under far more pressure.

The bookkeeper cost her roughly what a nice dinner costs, once a month, for six years. The time it saved at the finish line was worth vastly more than that. The peace of mind along the way was worth it on its own.

Months 1–3

Audit the ownership structure. Find every contractor who ever touched the product and confirm the agreement transferring their work to the company. Across the transactions Aegryn has been involved in or observed closely, the same three documents appear again and again as the ones that either accelerate a deal or stall it. They are not exciting. They are the kind of paperwork that feels like overhead until the moment someone needs them.

A signed IP assignment agreement for every contractor who ever touched the product. Not an NDA. Not a service agreement. A document that explicitly transfers ownership of whatever they built to the company. This is the single most common gap in European mid-market software transactions.

The gap has a typical shape. A freelance developer paid by invoice in year one. An agency that built the first version under a service agreement that never mentioned ownership. An open-source component whose licence obliges disclosure. None of it is malicious and almost all of it is fixable, provided it is found by the seller rather than by the buyer. Found by the buyer, the same three signatures become an escrow, a holdback or a closing that slips a quarter.

How to build one that works
The data room is
a first impression.
A data room tells a buyer something about a company before a single document is opened. A well-organised data room, clear folder structure, documents named consistently, no broken links, nothing missing, signals a management team that runs things properly. A data room with 200 randomly named files in a flat folder structure signals the opposite. The standard structure that works: four top-level folders, Financials, Customers, Technology, and Legal. Each subdivided simply. Financials contains monthly P&L for three years, plus any signed contracts with lenders. Build·The Art of the Data Room

The question founders ask most often is: how early should I build the data room? The answer is: today. Not because a buyer is waiting, but because the act of building a proper data room reveals every gap in how the company is documented. Finding those gaps when there is no buyer waiting costs nothing. Finding them in week two of due diligence costs trust, time, and often money.

The second most common question is: how detailed should it be? The answer is: complete and well-labelled, but not exhaustive in a way that creates confusion. A buyer needs to be able to find what they are looking for in under ten minutes. If the data room requires a guide to navigate, it is too complex.

The third question: who should see it? Access should be logged and controlled from the moment the room is created. Every person who opens a document should be tracked.

Four questions founders asked us in 2026

Value Desk.

The questions that came up most often in first conversations this year, and the answers we gave. Shortened, not softened.

"I read that companies like mine sell for six times revenue. Why is my first offer at three?"

Because the six was a median of disclosed deals, and disclosed deals skew large. Multiples scale with size, growth, retention and the number of bidders. A three at 100 percent ownership with no earn-out can be a better outcome than a six with a third of the price deferred. Compare the structure, not the headline.

"Should I raise a round first, to look bigger?"

Only if the round buys something a buyer will pay for: a second market, a product line, a management layer. Raising to inflate a valuation before a sale rarely works, because the acquirer prices your company, not your last round. The founders on pages 70 to 81 answered this question three different ways. All three were right for them.

"How long before I want to sell should I start preparing?"

Two years is comfortable. Twelve months is workable, and this issue describes exactly how to spend them. Six weeks is what most founders actually have, and it is why most first offers arrive below what the company is worth. Preparation is the only variable in a sale that costs nothing and is entirely within your control.

"Will a buyer keep my team?"

A strategic acquirer usually keeps the people it cannot replace and integrates the rest; a fund keeps almost everyone and changes the finance function first. Either way, the team a buyer values is the one that does not need you in the room. If every client relationship still runs through the founder, the buyer is not buying a team. He is buying you, and pricing the risk that you leave.

Money·The Bootstrap Path

For bootstrapped software companies in the three to ten million euro annual revenue range, realistic exit multiples run from three to five times yearly revenue, with equity-backed companies typically commanding a modest premium.

What separates a three-times outcome from a seven-times outcome comes down to three variables: growth rate, customer retention, and the efficiency score that combines both. Getting these right is the single highest-leverage activity a founder can focus on before going to market.

The three variables are worth defining plainly. Growth rate is the year-on-year increase in recurring revenue. Net revenue retention is what last year's customers are paying this year, after churn and expansion; above 100 percent, the base grows without a single new sale. The efficiency score most buyers use adds growth rate to profit margin: a company growing 25 percent at a 15 percent margin scores 40, the threshold at which the multiple conversation changes.

An earn-out is a payment structure where part of the price is paid after the transaction closes, contingent on the business hitting agreed targets. It lets a buyer pay more for future performance they cannot yet verify, and it lets a seller prove the business is worth what they claimed.

The risks are real. Targets can be set in ways that are difficult to achieve after the sale, especially if the founder is no longer in full operational control. The metrics used to calculate the earn-out should be simple, objective, and within the founder's ability to influence.

The market is moving towards them. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 records renewed growth in earn-outs across European private deals, a shift towards EBIT or EBITDA rather than revenue as the metric that triggers them, longer warranty periods and fewer low liability caps. Financial investors were 6 percent more present on the buy side than a year earlier, which adds weight to the trend. The direction is buyer-friendly, and a founder who has not prepared for it will meet it in the term sheet.

The founders who navigate earn-outs well have one advantage: they negotiated the terms before they signed, not after. Once the letter of intent is signed and exclusivity begins, the founder's leverage drops sharply.

The term "data room" sounds more intimidating than it is. At its core, it is a organised folder with documents that answer the questions a serious buyer will eventually ask. The founders who do well in a sale process are the ones who build this folder long before anyone asks for it.

There are four categories every buyer looks at, in roughly this order of importance: the financials, the customer contracts and revenue documentation, the technical architecture and code ownership, and the legal structure including shares, options, and any outstanding claims.

What the buyer reads first
Two questions,

The financials are usually the first thing opened. A buyer's first question is: can I trust this revenue? The second is: what is the cost of producing it? If those two questions can be answered quickly and clearly, the rest of the process tends to go faster.

"Trust the revenue" has a precise meaning. It means recurring rather than one-off, signed rather than verbal, and spread rather than concentrated: any customer above 20 percent of the total is priced as a risk. It means net revenue retention that can be reconstructed from invoices, not asserted in a deck. And it means monthly statements that reconcile to the bank, three years back, without a footnote explaining a change of method.

"Cost of producing it" is where founder dependency shows. A sales pipeline that only the founder has ever closed, a codebase only one engineer understands, a supplier contract that lapses on change of control: each is a cost the buyer will incur after closing, and each will appear, priced, in the offer.

Month two. Her first twelve nurses, all former colleagues, all on permanent contracts. Her accountant objected. She had seen what agency staffing did to continuity of care and to churn.

Month three. The first discharged patient coordinated end to end. A readmission avoided. One line in a report that two insurers read very carefully.

People·The First Eighteen Months
The first eighteen months.

The insurers paid on time. The hospital group did not refer a single patient for seven months, out of a caution she understood better than anyone: she had written the procurement policy it was applying to her. She kept going on two contracts and savings, and on the knowledge that the first hospital to refer would bring the others.

It did, in month eight, after a chief of medicine she had worked with for twelve years asked why his discharged patients kept coming back. By month eighteen she employed forty-one people, coordinated care in two cantons, and had turned down her first acquisition approach, from a private clinic group that wanted her referral network more than her company.

She still keeps the letter. Not as a trophy. As a reminder of what a buyer thought was valuable, eighteen months in, before she had thought to write it down herself.

The honest number Second-career founders are more common than the coverage suggests. Year two was organisation, not growth. She hired a finance director before a sales lead, wrote down the coordination protocol that had lived in her head, and put every nurse's client relationship into a system that did not depend on her remembering it. Three cantons by year three. One hundred and forty staff by year four.

It told her the company would survive her. That, she says, was the moment it became one.

Engineering services firm, Rhône-Alpes, 2019 to 2026. Anonymised.

The company had never been in trouble. Forty engineers, twelve years of profit, a reputation in industrial automation that brought work in without a sales team. Which is why nobody noticed that one automotive supplier had grown to 38 percent of revenue, or that the two partners who had founded the firm were the only people who had ever negotiated a contract.

When the client cut its programme in early 2024, the firm lost a third of its revenue in one quarter. The bank asked for a plan within sixty days. The partners had never written one.

People·What the Restructuring Taught Them

The restructuring itself was unremarkable: eleven departures, a renegotiated lease, a bridge facility. What changed the company was the diagnosis the bank forced them to write. Every capability that mattered lived in two heads. No engineer below partner level had a client relationship. There was no organisation chart because there had never needed to be one.

The pivot was organisational before it was commercial. They promoted three project leads to account owners with revenue targets, hired a finance director for the first time, and wrote down the delivery method the firm had used for a decade without ever describing it. Only then did they move into energy and water infrastructure, where the same automation skills were in demand and no client could exceed 15 percent of revenue by policy.

Two years later the firm is smaller than at its peak, more profitable than it ever was, and for the first time, sellable. Neither partner intends to sell. But both now know that the company would survive them, which turned out to be the point.

The same skills, a different market, and an organisation that no longer depends on two people.
The pattern Eurostat recorded falling business registrations and rising bankruptcies across the EU in the second quarter of 2026. Most restructurings are not caused by a bad product. They are caused by concentration: of clients, of knowledge, of signatures. The fix is organisational, and it is far cheaper when the bank is not yet in the room.

Life·Essay
Essay
On building
for the long view.
Aegryn Founding Team

There is a particular kind of ambition that the startup narrative does not celebrate, because it does not look good in a pitch deck. It is the ambition of building something that outlasts you, not just something that grows fast or exits well, but something that is genuinely better for having existed.

Europe is unusually good at it, and unusually quiet about it. Family-owned companies make up between 65 and 80 percent of all European businesses and employ 40 to 50 percent of the private workforce, according to European Family Businesses. Two thirds of Swiss SMEs describe themselves as family firms. Most of the companies that will still be trading in 2050 are already trading today, run by people who have never given an interview.

The founders who build for the long view often look, in the short term, like they are moving too slowly. They hire a finance director before they need one. They write down how the company works while it is small enough to describe. They refuse a customer who would be 40 percent of revenue. None of this compounds visibly in year two. All of it is visible in year ten, when a buyer, a bank or a successor opens the file and finds a company rather than a founder.

We called this issue Built to Last because the phrase is usually reserved for products. We think it belongs to organisations. The data in these pages, on survival, on succession, on what acquirers pay for and what they discount, points in one direction: the value of a company is, to a surprising degree, the legibility of what it has built. That is a choice, made early, and made again every year.

The long view, literally. Companies that last are built like valleys are carved: slowly, and by forces that do not need an audience.
129
Life·On Building for the Long View

The European technology market rewards a different profile of founder than the one most venture narratives celebrate. It rewards the person who keeps clean books because they believe in accuracy, not because a buyer might eventually check. The person who documents their code because they respect the next engineer who will read it, not because they are preparing for due diligence.

This is not naive. It is, in the end, the most practical approach. Companies built with this kind of care are the ones that survive a serious buyer's review, that close faster, that sell for more. The long view and the good outcome are not opposites. They are the same intention, expressed over different timeframes.

We write this magazine for people who already believe this, or who are beginning to. The work of building something real is not glamorous. Neither is the way this publication is made. But both, we think, hold up better than the alternative.

The Workspace of the Deliberate Founder
What they keep
on their desk.

The founders who build deliberately tend to have the same things on their desks. Not the flashiest hardware or the most subscriptions. One good notebook. A few well-chosen books with notes in the margins. A system for processing information that they have refined over years rather than adopted from a productivity blog last month.

The tools are secondary. What they are really protecting is the ability to think without interruption for four hours in the morning. That is the resource most founders underestimate, and the one most difficult to buy back once it has been given away. The workspace changes. The discipline behind it does not.

One notebook, paper. Decisions and the reasons for them, dated. Read again before every board meeting.

A protected morning. No meetings before eleven. The single most defended rule in every calendar we have seen.

A one-page company description. What it does, for whom, why it wins. Rewritten once a year. Handed to every new hire and, eventually, every buyer.

The number. Cash in the bank, months of runway, or net retention. One figure, checked weekly, never monthly.

Something unrelated to work. A rowing schedule, a woodworking bench, a language. Not a hobby. A place where the company cannot follow.

Not a library. A working collection.
