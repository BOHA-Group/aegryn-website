Primer·Five Words Worth Knowing
The vocabulary, plainly defined
Five words
worth knowing.
ARR

Annual Recurring Revenue. The yearly income a software company can reliably count on, from customers who pay every month or year. The number most buyers start from.

Multiple

How many years of revenue a buyer pays upfront. At 4× ARR, a buyer pays four times the annual revenue — all at once, today — for the right to own everything.

Due Diligence

The buyer's investigation of everything the seller has claimed. Financial records, code, legal ownership, customer contracts. The process that separates a fast close from a slow one.

Earn-Out

A portion of the sale price paid later, based on whether the business hits agreed targets after the sale closes. A bridge between what the buyer will pay today and what the seller believes the company is worth.

NRR

Net Revenue Retention. Whether existing customers spend more or less than last year. Above 100% means growth from the existing base alone. One of the strongest signals of a healthy business.

Tech and AI·What Actually Changed
The Freedom Ledger
Nine to five
is optional now.
The best ideas rarely arrive at a desk.

Ask any founder who built solo in the last two years what actually changed, and none of them mention artificial intelligence first. They mention their mornings. Their ability to take a Tuesday off. The fact that "office" now means wherever the wifi is reliable and the coffee is better.

The tools do the repetitive work, the first draft, the boilerplate, the parts of building a company that used to eat entire weeks. What is left for the founder is the part that was always the actual job: deciding what matters, talking to customers, and having the nerve to ship something before it is perfect.

Freedom, in this context, is not a slogan. It is a Tuesday afternoon spent by the pool with a laptop open, half-working and half-not, because for the first time the two are not opposites.

A Month-by-Month Account
Twelve months that
determine the price.

What follows is a distilled account of how a prepared founder uses the twelve months before a transaction. It is a composite of several real processes observed by Aegryn between 2023 and 2026. Not every step applies to every situation. The sequence, however, holds consistently.

Continued overleaf
51
Build·Twelve Months That Determine the Price
Continued

Months 1–3

Audit the ownership structure. Find every contractor who ever touched the product and confirm the agreement transferring their work to the company. This is the step most founders skip and most buyers ask about. It is almost always fixable, but it takes time to track down former contributors and obtain the right signatures. Starting early prevents panic later.

Months 4–6

Commission an independent financial review if the last one was more than eighteen months ago. Clean and consistent monthly financials for the past three years are one of the strongest signals a buyer receives about how a company is managed. The cost of the review is small relative to what it protects in the final offer.

Months 7–9

Document the technical architecture. Not for a buyer — for yourself first. Write down why each major component was built the way it was, what it depends on, and what would need to change if the core AI or infrastructure provider changed their pricing or API. This documentation takes less than a week for a small team to produce properly. It saves weeks in due diligence later.

Months 10–12

Organise the data room itself. Create a clear folder structure. Add a one-page executive summary that answers the buyer's first three questions before they ask. Rehearse the answers to the questions your own review revealed as potential concerns. By month twelve, the process should be the easiest part.

Build·The Three Documents
What closes deals faster than anything else
The three documents
that close deals.
The moment these documents make possible.

Across the transactions Aegryn has been involved in or observed closely, the same three documents appear again and again as the ones that either accelerate a deal or stall it. They are not exciting. They are the kind of paperwork that feels like overhead until the moment someone needs them.

A signed IP assignment agreement for every contractor who ever touched the product. Not an NDA. Not a service agreement. A document that explicitly transfers ownership of whatever they built to the company. This is the single most common gap in European mid-market software transactions.

Three years of independently reviewed monthly financial statements. Not annual accounts, not management accounts prepared by the founder. Monthly statements reviewed by someone who is not the founder, with margins and customer metrics visible by period. This document answers the buyer's first and most important question in the first afternoon of due diligence.

A one-page technical architecture summary. Written by the founder or lead engineer, explaining what the product is built on, what it depends on, and what would change if any of those dependencies changed. The best versions are written for a non-technical reader. They are three pages or fewer. They save weeks in technical due diligence.

Build·The Art of the Data Room
How to build one that works
The data room is
a first impression.
A data room tells a buyer something about a company before a single document is opened. A well-organised data room — clear folder structure, documents named consistently, no broken links, nothing missing — signals a management team that runs things properly. A data room with 200 randomly named files in a flat folder structure signals the opposite. The standard structure that works: four top-level folders — Financials, Customers, Technology, and Legal. Each subdivided simply. Financials contains monthly P&L for three years, plus any signed contracts with lenders. Customers contains the full customer list with ARR by client, plus copies of signed contracts with renewal terms. Technology contains the architecture summary, code ownership agreements, and any certifications or security reviews. Legal contains the shareholders agreement, option pool documentation, and corporate registration.
Continued overleaf
56
Build·The Art of the Data Room
Continued

The question founders ask most often is: how early should I build the data room? The answer is: today. Not because a buyer is waiting, but because the act of building a proper data room reveals every gap in how the company is documented. Finding those gaps when there is no buyer waiting costs nothing. Finding them in week two of due diligence costs trust, time, and often money.

The second most common question is: how detailed should it be? The answer is: complete and well-labelled, but not exhaustive in a way that creates confusion. A buyer needs to be able to find what they are looking for in under ten minutes. If the data room requires a guide to navigate, it is too complex.

The third question: who should see it? Access should be logged and controlled from the moment the room is created. Every person who opens a document should be tracked. This is not paranoia — it is standard practice, and it creates a record of who has seen what if the deal does not close and the information needs to be protected.

Money·Market Analysis
H1 2026
Fewer deals.
More concentrated capital.

European tech funding recovered to 44.1 billion euros in the first half of 2026, but across roughly 1,740 deals, well below the pace of 2024 when funding peaked at just over 50 billion. The market is not shrinking. It is concentrating. The UK raised 18.7 billion across 423 deals alone. Germany and France combined reached roughly the same. Capital is not evenly distributed, and it is not evenly distributed across company stages either. On the M&A side, strategic deal value in EMEA rose 77 percent through May as acquirers chose fewer, higher-conviction targets. The mid-market where most European founders operate is living inside that concentration, not above it.

Transaction·Getting Paid Later
The Part Nobody Explains Clearly
Getting paid
later.

An earn-out is a payment structure where part of the price is paid after the transaction closes, contingent on the business hitting agreed targets. It lets a buyer pay more for future performance they cannot yet verify, and it lets a seller prove the business is worth what they claimed.

The risks are real. Targets can be set in ways that are difficult to achieve after the sale, especially if the founder is no longer in full operational control. The metrics used to calculate the earn-out should be simple, objective, and within the founder's ability to influence.

The founders who navigate earn-outs well have one advantage: they negotiated the terms before they signed, not after. Once the letter of intent is signed and exclusivity begins, the founder's leverage drops sharply.

Transaction·The Data Room
The Document That Closes Deals
What a data room
actually contains.

The term "data room" sounds more intimidating than it is. At its core, it is a organised folder with documents that answer the questions a serious buyer will eventually ask. The founders who do well in a sale process are the ones who build this folder long before anyone asks for it.

There are four categories every buyer looks at, in roughly this order of importance: the financials, the customer contracts and revenue documentation, the technical architecture and code ownership, and the legal structure including shares, options, and any outstanding claims.

The financials are usually the first thing opened. A buyer's first question is: can I trust this revenue? The second is: what is the cost of producing it? If those two questions can be answered quickly and clearly, the rest of the process tends to go faster.

Continued overleaf
88
Transaction·What a Data Room Contains
Continued
The financial folder should contain monthly recurring revenue by customer for the last three years, a profit and loss statement for the same period, a cash flow summary, and any outstanding debt or commitments. This is not exotic. These are the documents any founder should be able to produce in an afternoon if their books are kept properly. The customer section is where many founders are caught off guard. A buyer wants to see signed contracts, not verbal agreements or email threads. They want to see the length of each contract, the renewal terms, and whether any single customer represents more than 20 percent of total revenue. Concentration is a risk, and buyers price it into their offers. The technical section often takes the longest to prepare if it was not maintained as the product was built. Code ownership agreements for every contractor, documentation of what each major component does and why it was built that way, and a clear picture of what dependencies the product relies on — these are the documents that technical reviewers look for first. Their absence does not kill a deal, but their presence saves weeks. The legal folder is the most varied. Shareholders agreement, option pool documentation, any existing litigation or regulatory notices, and the corporate structure across jurisdictions. Most European mid-market deals fail to close because of something in this folder that was not disclosed early — and that cost the founder the time and trust they spent building the relationship.

What an earn-out
actually feels like.

He sold his company for what looked like a good headline number. A third was paid on closing. A third would be paid twelve months later if the company hit its annual recurring revenue target. What matters is what the presenting partner chose to highlight. Which means what matters, long before the committee, is the data room the seller built before the process started.

Lausanne and Geneva

The triangle of Lausanne, Geneva, and the EPFL campus is one of Europe's quietest concentrations of serious engineering talent. The quality of life is remarkable. The cost is high by European standards, but the proximity to capital, to talent, and to a culture that takes precision seriously makes it worth it for the right kind of company.

Lisbon

Still the most accessible entry point into a founder community for someone moving to Europe alone. English-speaking, architecturally beautiful, and cheap enough that a slow month does not become a crisis. Rents have risen sharply but remain a fraction of London or Zurich.

Berlin

Unglamorous, large, cheap enough to take real risks. A city where nobody cares particularly what you are building, which is exactly what some founders need.

Stockholm

The city that produced more billion-dollar technology companies per capita than anywhere outside Silicon Valley. Something in the combination of pragmatism, design sensibility, and long winters produces a particular kind of builder.

There is a particular kind of ambition that the startup narrative does not celebrate, because it does not look good in a pitch deck. Life·On Building for the Long View
Continued

The European technology market rewards a different profile of founder than the one most venture narratives celebrate. It rewards the person who keeps clean books because they believe in accuracy, not because a buyer might eventually check. The person who documents their code because they respect the next engineer who will read it, not because they are preparing for due diligence.

This is not naive. It is, in the end, the most practical approach. Companies built with this kind of care are the ones that survive a serious buyer's review, that close faster, that sell for more. The long view and the good outcome are not opposites. They are the same intention, expressed over different timeframes.

We write this magazine for people who already believe this — or who are beginning to. The work of building something real is not glamorous. Neither is the way this publication is made. But both, we think, hold up better than the alternative.

Life·The Founder Stack
The Workspace of the Deliberate Founder
What they keep
on their desk.
Not a library. A working collection.

The founders who build deliberately tend to have the same things on their desks. Not the flashiest hardware or the most subscriptions. One good notebook. A few well-chosen books with notes in the margins. A system for processing information that they have refined over years rather than adopted from a productivity blog last month.

The tools are secondary. What they are really protecting is the ability to think without interruption for four hours in the morning. That is the resource most founders underestimate, and the one most difficult to buy back once it has been given away.

The workspace changes. The discipline behind it does not.
