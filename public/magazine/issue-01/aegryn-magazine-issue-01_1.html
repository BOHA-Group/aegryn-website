From the Founding Team
Built to be
acquired.

This magazine began as a question we kept asking ourselves: why do so many solid European technology companies sell for far less than they are worth, or fail to sell at all?

The answer was almost never the product. It was preparation. Documentation. The kind of unglamorous, systematic work that founders put off until a buyer is already asking for it.

We built Aegryn to change that. This magazine is where we tell the stories, share the data, and publish the opinions that we believe this market needs but rarely gets from someone without a conflict of interest.

Build·Twelve Months That Determine the Price
Continued

Months 1–3

Audit the ownership structure. Find every contractor who ever touched the product and confirm the agreement transferring their work to the company. This is the step most founders skip and most buyers ask about. It is almost always fixable, but it takes time to track down former contributors and obtain the right signatures. Starting early prevents panic later.

Months 4–6

Commission an independent financial review if the last one was more than eighteen months ago. Clean and consistent monthly financials for the past three years are one of the strongest signals a buyer receives about how a company is managed. The cost of the review is small relative to what it protects in the final offer.

Months 7–9

Document the technical architecture. Not for a buyer — for yourself first. Write down why each major component was built the way it was, what it depends on, and what would need to change if the core AI or infrastructure provider changed their pricing or API. This documentation takes less than a week for a small team to produce properly. It saves weeks in due diligence later.

Months 10–12

Organise the data room itself. Create a clear folder structure. Add a one-page executive summary that answers the buyer's first three questions before they ask. Rehearse the answers to the questions your own review revealed as potential concerns. By month twelve, the process should be the easiest part.

Build·He Had Twelve Months
Continued

He started with the IP. He found three former contractors whose work was in the product's core module, went back to each of them, explained what he needed, and obtained the assignment agreements in the first six weeks. One former contractor asked for a small payment in exchange. He paid it without argument. The alternative was a gap in ownership that would have derailed the sale entirely.

He commissioned a financial review in month four. The accountant found two revenue recognition issues that, while not material to the business's health, would have required explanation in due diligence. Fixed before anyone was looking, they cost a few days. Discovered by a buyer's auditor, they would have cost weeks and a price adjustment.

He documented the technical architecture in month seven. Three pages, written by him over two weekends. What each major component does, why it was built that way, and what the fallback would be if the primary AI provider raised its prices or shut down. A buyer's technical reviewer later told him it was the clearest technical summary he had seen in two years of doing deals.

When the acquirer called back in month thirteen — slightly late — his data room was complete. The due diligence took six weeks. The deal closed in four months from first formal conversation. Both sides described it as the smoothest process they had been part of.

Build·The Art of the Data Room
How to build one that works
The data room is
a first impression.
A data room tells a buyer something about a company before a single document is opened. A well-organised data room — clear folder structure, documents named consistently, no broken links, nothing missing — signals a management team that runs things properly. A data room with 200 randomly named files in a flat folder structure signals the opposite. The standard structure that works: four top-level folders — Financials, Customers, Technology, and Legal. Each subdivided simply. Financials contains monthly P&L for three years, plus any signed contracts with lenders. Customers contains the full customer list with ARR by client, plus copies of signed contracts with renewal terms. Technology contains the architecture summary, code ownership agreements, and any certifications or security reviews. Legal contains the shareholders agreement, option pool documentation, and corporate registration.

Money·What a Sale Actually Looks Like

The private mid-market for software companies sits at a median around 4.5 times yearly revenue in 2026, with a range from three to seven times for most transactions. But the gap between the bottom and the top of that range has widened sharply since 2022. The variable is almost always preparation. A ten-point improvement in net revenue retention now translates into a 20 to 30 percent valuation increase. Companies that are profitable in 2026 command stronger multiples than fast-growing but unprofitable ones at the same overall efficiency score. This is a direct reversal of the logic that ruled two years earlier. European software companies still trade at a discount of 15 to 25 percent versus comparable US businesses. That gap has narrowed from 30 to 40 percent five years ago, but it has not closed.

Money·What the EMEA Wave Means
For a founder with €2M in annual revenue
What the consolidation

The 77 percent rise in EMEA strategic deal value is a real number, but it is a number about a market. It does not automatically translate to a better outcome for any specific founder. What it does create — and what was less true two years ago — is a wider set of credible buyers for mid-sized European technology assets.

Strategic acquirers are now more willing to look at assets below ten million euros in annual recurring revenue than they were during the tighter capital environment of 2022 and 2023. This is partly regulatory — the Draghi report's influence on how European companies think about scale — and partly competitive: American companies are deploying more capital in Europe, which is forcing European incumbents to respond with acquisitions rather than organic investment.

Where the capital is moving. And where it is not.

The practical implication is simple: a well-prepared asset at two million euros in annual recurring revenue now has access to a buyer pool that, five years ago, would have required at least five million. The preparation requirement has not changed. The universe of potential buyers for a prepared asset has expanded.

Transaction·The Process
From First Meeting to Signature
What actually happens,
week by week.
The first meeting: 45 minutes, three questions, a decision already forming.

The first conversation is rarely decisive. The buyer is pattern-matching against dozens of companies they have seen. What makes a founder memorable is having a clear answer to the three questions every buyer is actually asking: can I trust the revenue, can I run this without the founder, and is there a real reason I should pay more than I would for something similar?

The letter of intent arrives after roughly four to eight weeks of conversation, depending on whether the process is competitive. The exclusivity period that follows is when the real work happens.

Transaction·Getting Paid Later
The Part Nobody Explains Clearly
Getting paid
later.

An earn-out is a payment structure where part of the price is paid after the transaction closes, contingent on the business hitting agreed targets. It lets a buyer pay more for future performance they cannot yet verify, and it lets a seller prove the business is worth what they claimed.

The risks are real. Targets can be set in ways that are difficult to achieve after the sale, especially if the founder is no longer in full operational control. The metrics used to calculate the earn-out should be simple, objective, and within the founder's ability to influence.

The founders who navigate earn-outs well have one advantage: they negotiated the terms before they signed, not after. Once the letter of intent is signed and exclusivity begins, the founder's leverage drops sharply.

Transaction·What a Data Room Contains
Continued
The financial folder should contain monthly recurring revenue by customer for the last three years, a profit and loss statement for the same period, a cash flow summary, and any outstanding debt or commitments. This is not exotic. These are the documents any founder should be able to produce in an afternoon if their books are kept properly. The customer section is where many founders are caught off guard. A buyer wants to see signed contracts, not verbal agreements or email threads. They want to see the length of each contract, the renewal terms, and whether any single customer represents more than 20 percent of total revenue. Concentration is a risk, and buyers price it into their offers. The technical section often takes the longest to prepare if it was not maintained as the product was built. Code ownership agreements for every contractor, documentation of what each major component does and why it was built that way, and a clear picture of what dependencies the product relies on — these are the documents that technical reviewers look for first. Their absence does not kill a deal, but their presence saves weeks. The legal folder is the most varied. Shareholders agreement, option pool documentation, any existing litigation or regulatory notices, and the corporate structure across jurisdictions. Most European mid-market deals fail to close because of something in this folder that was not disclosed early — and that cost the founder the time and trust they spent building the relationship.

Transaction·First 48 Hours
Continued

By Wednesday afternoon, the analyst has a preliminary view. If the financials hold up to the summary, he moves to customer contracts. He is counting the number of contracts that are signed versus the number that are verbal. He is looking at contract lengths. He is asking whether any single customer accounts for more than 20 percent of annual revenue — because if they do, that customer is a risk, and risk is priced into the offer.

By Thursday, the technical reviewer has a first look at the code and architecture documentation. This is where the 48-hour picture shifts for prepared versus unprepared founders. In a well-documented data room, the reviewer can build a mental model of the product's defensibility in two hours. In an unprepared one, they are still asking basic questions about ownership and dependency at the end of the week.

Week one. Every question asked now shapes the final offer.
The week-one signal The pace of week one in due diligence is the strongest predictor of deal outcome. Founders who answer questions in hours, not days, close faster and at higher prices. Preparation is speed. Speed is trust. Trust is price.

Transaction·Point of View
Aegryn Point of View
The certification
that changes
the conversation.
Aegryn Founding Team

The most common question we hear from founders who have just completed their CIFS grade is: does this change what I can ask for? The honest answer is: sometimes directly, and almost always indirectly.

Directly, a certified asset eliminates a category of buyer uncertainty. The conversation shifts from "can we trust these numbers and this documentation" to "do we want to own this specific company." That is a different and more productive conversation, and it tends to happen faster.

Indirectly, certification changes who approaches you. The compliance officer who found the RegTech company through an approved vendor registry was not the only buyer of that type active in 2026. There is a growing category of buyer — particularly in regulated industries — who searches for certified assets specifically, because the certification reduces their own post-acquisition remediation cost and timeline.

We grade assets because we believe verification is the foundation of a fair transaction. We are also pragmatic: a grade makes the process faster, the conversations better, and the outcomes more predictable. Those are good things for sellers and for buyers alike.

The Workspace of the Deliberate Founder
What they keep
on their desk.
Not a library. A working collection.

The founders who build deliberately tend to have the same things on their desks. Not the flashiest hardware or the most subscriptions. One good notebook. A few well-chosen books with notes in the margins. A system for processing information that they have refined over years rather than adopted from a productivity blog last month.

The tools are secondary. What they are really protecting is the ability to think without interruption for four hours in the morning. That is the resource most founders underestimate, and the one most difficult to buy back once it has been given away.

The workspace changes. The discipline behind it does not.
